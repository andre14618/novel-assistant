The door let in a shove of cold air and shut it out. The hall smelled of wet wool, cheap ink, and the yeast-sour breath of men who'd started drinking before the bells. Kael worked through the crowd, elbows tight, the weight of his empty purse counted on the walk to the posting board — two spans of cork over iron, warped from a hundred thumbs pressing holes into it.

He stopped at the rail.

The date-line read Sixth day. He counted on his fingers like every morning this week. Seventh. Eighth. Ninth. Tenth. Four days until the bell rang over the guild market and Mira's marker went up beside the unsold fish and the dead men's tools.

The board did not care.

He scanned the columns the way a man scans river ice, hunting the place that would hold weight. Cartage, Rillgate to Aldertree: pays after the autumn fair. Dock salvage: settle at month's end. Locksmith's mate: wages on completion. Every one of them read after the tenth. The lower boards held the same work at worse numbers, flyers so old rain had soaked the ink into the cork. Nothing paid before Mira's marker sold.

His eyes came back to the top corner. The one posting with a paid-in-hand stamp red as a wound.

Gray Salt Mine. Deep salvage. The flyer hung behind a twist of black iron thread, the kind guild threshers wove into contracts when they wanted to be certain of a man. Kael put two fingers against it. Cold, the way cold metal stays cold, however warm the hall.

The terms: recover the surveyor's chest from the lower gallery, flooded after the spring collapse. No breathing apparatus. Pay on signature, in silver, before he ever set foot in the shaft. Iron-thread binding held the terms and the forfeits. And three names below, each crossed through with a single clean line. Men who had signed the same thread and drowned for it. The ink over them was darker than the rest, as if whoever crossed them had pressed hard.

The silver would buy Mira's marker four times over. The shaft had silt and cold dark and three dead men in its ledger, and the thread would take what it took if he failed it.

He could sign. Thumb the inked square, walk to the pay clerk, count silver before noon. Or dig through the ruined postings on the lower boards. Or ask.

The keeper of the records sat in the back corner under a single lamp, scraping at his ledger with a bone-handled knife. Orin. Kael had never once asked him anything. Orin had never once looked up.

But the board did not say what happened to the silver if the binder drowned in the lower gallery. Whether the thread clawed the payment back out of a corpse. Out of the next of kin. Whether a dead man's contract could call in a living girl's marker four days from the bell. That was a question for the books, not the cork.

Four days. Kael took his hand off the iron thread and let the cold go.

He walked the length of the board. At the end he turned — not toward the pay clerk's window, not back to the lower boards — but across the hall to the records desk, with the Gray Salt question in his mouth and no contract in his hands.

The Contract Hall smelled of lamp oil, dried glue, and the particular rot of paper kept too long in damp. Kael Rusk threaded between the tall desks toward the back corner where Orin Vale worked, a thin clerk whose sleeves had been rolled to the elbow so long the creases had set. Orin had his nose in an open ledger, a junior scribe at his elbow with a pile of folios. He glanced up at Kael's approach and waved the boy off.

"Rank law," Kael said, low. The kind of voice men used when the walls had ears and the ears had prices. "Can it make the Gray Salt contract hold for Mira without a witness?"

Orin's hands stilled. He set down his pen, squared it against the ledger's spine, and only then looked up. "Rank law doesn't make anything hold," he said. "Rank law is a door. You walk through it and the Hall remembers your feet. You don't, and you're standing outside in the rain."

"I'm standing outside in the rain now."

"That you are." Orin dragged a clean sheet toward him and drew a line down its middle with a thumbnail. "Admissible evidence. Three kinds. One: an oath-taker's witness mark — the seal of a bonded singer, sworn to speak true, paid by the hour. Do you have a witness mark?"

"You know I don't."

"Two: a sealsman's ledger entry. The Hall's own record of a signing, made same-day, in Hall ink, with Hall wax. Do you have a sealsman's entry?"

Kael shook his head once. The signing had happened in the Salt's back room, quick and quiet, a favor to a man who owed him. No clerk, no ledger, nothing but cheap wet ink and Mira's hand on the page.

"Three." The thumbnail tapped the right side of the line. "Bronze rank on the claimant. A merchant, a freeholder, a guild master — someone the Hall trusts to be worth something when the word comes due. They sign, they avow, they're in the book. After that, a creditor has to argue with the ledger instead of with you."

"And Mira?"

"Mira was the signer on a dead-word, I take it. No witness, no entry, no rank." Orin set the pen down again, exact and careful. "A dead-word is testimony. It's what you heard. The Hall doesn't take testimony from men who happened to be at the table. It takes evidence from men paid to be there. Your presence is a memory, Kael. A memory is not a mark."

Kael's hands lay flat on the desk. He could feel the grain through old calluses, and under that, the weight of every job he'd hauled to keep Mira clear of the creditor's reach. "So the dead-word stays dead unless a bronze stands up and avows it."

"In Contract Hall, before the seal, the full price named, the signing recorded. Then it lives." Orin met his eyes without the clerk's distance this time. "The rank is the door. You buy one, earn one, or borrow a name that already carries one. There are no back windows on this, Kael. The Hall only opens doors."

"Who opens before noon?"

Orin hesitated. The lamp guttered; somewhere a door banged shut and a junior scribe hurried past, folios smelling of fresh wax. When Orin spoke again it was quieter. "Lady Varn keeps a desk in the north aisle on workdays. Bronze by marriage, freeholder in her own name, and she takes avowals for a fee when the mood holds. Her address is with the doorman. The fee is real, and the time is realer." He picked up his pen, dipped it, paused. "You've got maybe a day before the creditor reads the terms and cries it up in this Hall. A day is a short rope to hang a door on."

Kael straightened. The weight sat in his shoulders, in the flat of his back where the work-slips always landed. "Fee's real. Address is real. That's more than I had this morning."

"Then you're richer." Orin touched the nib to the ledger. "One thing. When you find your bronze — and you will need one, there is no other way through — you'll stand at that seal and put your name on the same line. Whatever else that contract is, it's about to carry your mark. The law protects Mira when you do that. It stops protecting you."

Kael looked down at his palms. The calluses held the shape of every rope he'd pulled, every cart he'd hauled, every job done in the low places where rank never mattered. "She doesn't get sold," he said, "and I don't get hauled. I'll be at that seal before the creditor's man finishes his breakfast."

Orin nodded once and returned to his figures. Kael turned and crossed the Hall toward the door, toward the doorman, toward an address that cost coin he didn't have and a bronze name he would have to buy or earn or steal before the rope ran out.

The stall smelled of ink and old copper. A sheet of patched iron rattled overhead when the wind worked between the booths, and the floorboards never quite met — coins had a way of finding the cracks. Kael had stood under this roof once with his father, back when Hask was a man you called on, not a man you owed.

Hask did not rise at his step. The quill scratched one last line into the open ledger before his eyes lifted. Small, flat eyes, pale as farthings. "You're the brother. Mira's."

"Her name is on your page." Kael kept his voice level, the way calm water stayed level before a gale. "I want terms on the sale date."

"The bell rings the fourteenth-day. Eight sharp. Counter bid takes the marker, and the debt becomes his property." Hask said it the way a priest reads scripture — unchanged and old. "That is the market's way."

"And if the date moves?"

Something surfaced in Hask's face that was not a price but close kin to it. He touched the ledger's corner the way other men touched a weapon. "Time is a product. It has weight."

"How much?"

"A sixth of the principal. Paid up front. The bell waits for money, son — never for grief, and never for rank."

Kael had a rank. Had a name, a flag, a stretch of sea he'd bled for. He could have emptied all of it onto the counter, and Hask would have written it in the ledger's margin and charged him for the ink. Kael's jaw worked. He imagined the ledger's spine cracking under his knuckles, the stall coming down around them, the whole district burning honest fire — and let the thought burn out to nothing. A threat here wouldn't move the bell. It would only sharpen the price.

"A sixth," he repeated. "Then the sale moves."

"Twenty days more. No second extension. When I give weight to time, I give it once." Hask turned the ledger around, just far enough for Kael to read the line. Mira's name. Her mark. The sale date, and after it, a blank space where a buyer's name would sit. "You'll know the buyer when the bell rings. Until then, his name is not yours to ask."

"The coin is the only word you answer to?"

"The only word that doesn't lie." Hask snapped the ledger shut, and the sound was the whole district in miniature — final, flat, unopenable.

Kael turned before his hands made a decision his purse couldn't back, and walked out through the awning's shadow into cold daylight. The coin-smell chased him to the corner, and there he understood what Hask had priced: not Mira's debt. The deadline. And that price, he could not pay twice.

So the only lever left was the earning — the days stacking between now and the fourteenth-day, the bell waiting at the far end like a moored ship that weighed anchor on schedule. He had a deadline he could work. And he had a buyer's name that would never be his unless he reached the bell first. No creditor would ever wait for his rank. The coin, and only the coin, would make the market answer.

The hall had gone stale. The air carried the day's last traffic—wax cracked under impatient thumbs, nibs scratching across indemnity forms, the senior records clerk turning ledger pages with the flat tread of a man who'd heard every lie a contract could hide. Kael stood at the postings wall and read the Gray Salt run for the fourth time.

The numbers were honest, which was the first thing to make him suspicious. Pay on delivery, half advanced at the counter, the route running low along the Gray Salt road—no patrols past the second ford, because the guard rotation didn't reach that far, and nobody who worked the road pressed for an explanation of why. He traced the terms a second time. It was a haul contract, not a bounty. That meant the law could enforce one thing: the fee. Everything else was the open road's business, and the open road didn't arbitrate disputes or send condolences to next of kin.

His hand drifted to the signing dish on the counter. The wax was fresh, the stick still warm from the seal-press. He didn't take it.

He'd sorted the fast-pay postings all over again, top to bottom, the way a man sorts a hand he already knows is dead. Courier work to the coastal ports—required two years' bonded rank, and the Hall's own registrar could prove he'd spent those years in a debt cell. Recovery of a marked ledger out in the salt flats—required a licensed witness to verify the seal, and the witness fee swallowed half the find. Dock-load disputes—three copper under the day rate, and an arbitration clause buried so deep in the small print that a man had to lose a thumb before it ever got read aloud.

The wall went dark at the edges. The Gray Salt run stayed lit.

It was the only posting that fit the timeline. Departure at first light, delivery before the bell. If the bell found him standing in this hall with nothing signed, the collectors would find him within the hour—the law had names for men who owed, and not one of them was kind. A haul contract put him on the road, and the road was the one place a debt could be outrun. The law could back that. The law could not back a man standing still.

He stepped back from the wall.

Lady Varn. The lead had cost him a drink and a day's patience—a name on a slip behind the second counter, passed over because the broker owed favors in three directions and was trying to pay them all with someone else's time. Word said she needed a man who could read a contract's fine print and keep his mouth shut about what the fine print meant. That work paid slower. It might pay better. It might pay in promises, and promises were a currency the bell didn't accept.

The Gray Salt run was safe. That was the trap of it. Safe meant the guard captain read the route manifest over his morning tea. Safe meant a stamped contract, a numbered copy in the Hall's deep files, and a paper trail any enemy with a copper and a grudge could follow. The posting said "discreet." Anything written down is a witness. The road was the only thing that didn't talk back.

He could walk the lower streets instead and gamble that a private arrangement surfaced before the bell—handshake work, no ledger, no name on wax. But handshakes paid in bruises more often than coin, and the bell didn't negotiate.

He could sign, of course. It was the clean answer, the one that didn't require trusting a name on a slip of paper. And it was the same thing he'd done every time the world had cornered him: taken the offered door because the wall behind him was closing in.

The safety of having no choice wasn't safety. It was just a slower version of the bell.

His fingers uncurled. The contract stayed pinned, unsigned, wax cooling in the dish. He'd go to Varn's door first and read her fine print before he committed his name to the road. If she was wrong, the wall would still be there—but it would be his choice to walk toward it, not the world's shove.

The hall door pulled shut behind him. Evening air off the river met him, carrying lamp-smoke and, thin and distant, the first stirring of the bell's hour.

The doors of the Contract Hall shut behind him with a sound like a ledger closing. Evening had gutted the street — the fruit carts folded, the lanterns lit, a junior clerk scurrying past with folios hugged to his chest, head down against nothing in particular. Kael stood at the top of the steps with Lady Varn's address crimped between two fingers and the contract folded in his fist, unsigned, bearing no seal, carrying no weight in the world except the weight he gave it.

He read the address again, though he'd already memorized it on the way to the door. Copper-row tenement, third floor, bellpull marked with a wax seal. The clerk who'd handed it over had been apologetic in that specific way clerks are when they know they're condemning someone to a meeting. Kael had asked for something else — anything else — and the clerk had shown him the empty board, the shuttered windows of the Stewards' office at fourth bell, the guild assessor gone to some country estate until after winter's first freeze. The Gray Salt run sat ranked below the priority counter, too far down a list he didn't have the clearance to climb. Every door closed by rank or time. This scrap of paper was what remained.

The address was supposed to be a lead. It wasn't. It was an ultimatum dressed in good penmanship.

Meet Lady Varn at the copper-row tonight, get the answers the Hall wouldn't sell him, and risk whatever price a broker charged for sitting in her chair. Sign the Gray Salt run first, take the contract, get the guild's protection under his name, and come back to an address whose patience had a shelf life. Or walk. Just walk, and let Mira's marker leave guild protection, and let her name pass into some collector's ledger where his hands couldn't reach it no matter how hard he clawed.

He turned the address over. The back was blank, and somehow that blankness was worse than ink.

The safety of walking away was a real thing. He could feel it in the soles of his boots — the simple arithmetic of not going, of waking up tomorrow owing no one new, of letting the copper-row keep its secrets. Whatever Lady Varn wanted, it had a price, and prices in the copper-row were paid in more than coin. The contract in his fist had been waiting for his signature at the bottom, that blank line the easiest place in the city to stand, and he wasn't standing there.

Kael tucked the contract inside his coat. He kept the address in his hand.

Third floor, wax seal, a broker who'd sent to find him before he'd even left the Hall. He didn't know which of those was the trap and which was the door. The street stretched out ahead of him, lamplight pooling on the cobbles, and beyond the lamplight the evening had teeth.

He stepped off the steps and went toward it.
